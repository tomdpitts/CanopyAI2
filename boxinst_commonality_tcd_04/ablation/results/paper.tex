\abstract{Individual tree crown segmentation from aerial imagery underpins tree-level carbon accounting, biodiversity, and restoration monitoring at landscape scale. However, existing models are predominantly trained on dense canopy forest imagery and degrade in savannah and drylands, where tree crowns are sparse, of variable appearance, and underrepresented in annotated benchmarks. We introduce LACE (LAtent Commonality Expectation-maximisation), a box-supervised instance segmentation model, evaluated on $0.1$ m/px aerial RGB tree crown imagery. LACE uses a frozen DINOv3-web ViT-L/16 encoder, applied at shifted offsets and interlaced into a dense feature grid, with a DINO-DETR detection head trained solely on bounding boxes. We use expectation-\hspace{0pt}maximisation to separate recurring appearance, the ``treeness'', within bounding boxes from surroundings. On the OAM-TCD benchmark test set, LACE reaches a mask AP$_{50}$ of $0.714$ trained on $900$ box-annotated images, ahead of BoxTeacher with $0.664$ and Restor's released mask-supervised Mask R-CNN model that achieves $0.648$ with full ${\sim}4.2$k image set polygon mask training.  By leveraging frozen self-supervised features, LACE matches fully-supervised specialist baselines from boxes alone, removing the need for polygon annotation in tree crown instance segmentation for sparse-canopy environments where labelled data is scarce.}

\keyword{Tree crown instance segmentation, self-supervised vision transformers, remote sensing, carbon accounting, DINOv3, expectation-\hspace{0pt}maximisation, box-supervised instance segmentation}

\section{Introduction}


Accurate measurement of individual tree crowns can serve as a proxy for woody biomass estimation via allometries~\cite{hiernaux2023,tucker2023} and thereby enable monitoring of carbon sequestration within areas containing trees. Measurement techniques in this domain have traditionally focused on dense canopy areas and forests, but there is an increasing body of literature that highlights the limitations of this approach as distribution, density, cover, and carbon content of trees in sparse-canopy biomes (listed in Table~\ref{tab:sparse-biomes}) are not well understood at sub-continental to continental scales~\cite{tucker2023,selvabox,fagan2020}. Recent studies highlight that the global contribution of sparse-canopy biomes as a carbon sink is underestimated due to the underestimation of tree mass in sparse-canopy areas~\cite{bastin2017,tucker2023,brandt2020,skole2021}, even while semi-arid ecosystems account for roughly half of the trend in the global land carbon sink and $39$--$47\%$ of its interannual variability~\cite{ahlstrom2015}. At the same time, 1/4 of all trees globally are located in grassland, savannah, desert and tundra biomes~\cite{crowther2015}. Drylands cover ${\sim}41.5\%$ of the Earth's land surface~\cite{sorensen2007,bastin2017} and are expanding further: they are projected to cover $50\%$ or more by 2100~\cite{huang2016}.


Machine learning and deep learning applied to satellite and UAV data have shifted the field from aggregate cover fractions toward mapping individual trees: Brandt et al.~\cite{brandt2020} mapped over $1.8$ billion crowns across $1.3$ million $\mathrm{km}^2$ of the Sahara and Sahel from $0.5$~m imagery. Tucker et al.~\cite{tucker2023} extended this to carbon estimation over $9.9$ billion individual trees. Mugabowindekwe et al.~\cite{mugabowindekwe2023} produced nation-wide tree-level carbon estimates for Rwanda using $0.25$~m/px imagery, and found that $48.6\%$ of aboveground tree carbon stock was located outside forests. They also found that ``even a very detailed manual \emph{forest} delineation\ldots missed $38.4\%$ of the isolated trees in Rwanda, which account for $25.5\%$ of the national aboveground carbon stocks'' (emphasis added). These studies establish that \emph{individual} crown detection in open, scattered-tree environments is tractable, but, crucially, typically depend on a) (often proprietary) sub-metre-resolution imagery and b) labelled data, motivating more transferable and label-efficient methods.

Table~\ref{tab:gap} (Appendix~\ref{app:gap}) summarises representative deep-learning methods for tree crown instance segmentation (TCIS) on RGB imagery. We address three gaps. Firstly, existing approaches focus on dense-canopy forests. For example, the authors of Detectree2 deliberately exclude images with $\leq 40\%$ canopy cover~\cite{ball2023}, and in the seminal DeepForest paper the authors highlight the worst-performing site, Onaqui, Utah, as a ``desert scrub site with a different vegetation structure from any of the training data''~\cite{weinstein2020}. Our setting is instead savannah, scrub and other open landscapes, where crowns are sparse, small and set against bare or grassy ground (Figure~\ref{fig:qual}). Secondly, existing models typically rely on manually annotated polygons for training~\cite{hao2021,braga2020,ocer2020,ball2023,chadwick2020,zhao2023}. Instead, we use boxes only, which have the benefit of being much faster and therefore cheaper to produce~\cite{jain2013}. Thirdly, apples-to-apples comparison across this literature remains difficult, as papers variously report AP$_{50}$, AP$_{50:95}$, instance- or semantic-F1 at differing IoU thresholds against boxes and/or polygons. The most widely used matching criterion in TCIS is an IoU $\geq0.5$ between predicted and ground truth (GT) crowns~\cite{ball2023,weinstein2020,hao2021,plesoianu2020}, and we adopt it for our headline metrics, mask AP$_{50}$ and F1.

\paragraph{Lack of sparse-canopy datasets}
We see two contributing factors to the exclusion of sparse-canopy biomes. The first is spatial resolution: ITC requires high ($<1$~m/px) or ultra-high ($\leq 0.1$~m/px) imagery~\cite{oamtcd}, beyond most publicly available satellite RGB at $5$--$10$~m/px. Downsampling the OAM-TCD holdout set used in this work ($439$ images, $25{,}705$ crowns) from its native $0.1$~m/px to a $1$~m Ground Sample Distance (GSD) leaves $25.0\%$ of crowns enclosing no complete pixel. The second is that conventional global tree cover maps such as Hansen et al.~\cite{hansen2013} systematically underestimate tree cover in drylands~\cite{fagan2020} and are markedly less accurate outside closed-canopy forests~\cite{brandtstolle2021}, while biomass and carbon products used for drylands~\cite{baccini2012,avitabile2016,bouvet2018} typically apply a single method to forest and dryland vegetation alike~\cite{tucker2023}. We speculate that this has led to a subtle vicious cycle: under-counting makes woody biomass in sparse-canopy biomes appear too scarce to justify annotation effort, which in turn stymies tree crown detection and segmentation in exactly the biomes where cover is most uncertain~\cite{bastin2017}. Tucker et al.~\cite{tucker2023} found that ``areas with scattered trees are often represented by zero values'', since ``previous models are rarely developed, trained and validated with plots of very sparse tree cover''. Since existing models all rely on supervised learning, datasets precede models. Things are changing, however: SelvaBox~\cite{selvabox} and SelvaMask~\cite{selvamask} have broadened geographic coverage of dense-canopy annotations, and OAM-TCD~\cite{oamtcd} includes a variety of biomes, including urban, tundra and savannah.

\paragraph{Box- and Mask-supervised learning}


Apart from limitations within sparse-canopy coverage, TCIS also remains understudied due to the lack of individual tree crown mask annotations in datasets~\cite{teng2025}. Since crown polygons are slower and typically more expensive to annotate than boxes, box-supervised instance segmentation~\cite{boxinst,box2mask,boxteacher} offers a practical route to instance-level delineation without polygon labels. The recent rise of self-supervised backbones such as DINOv2~\cite{dinov2} and DINOv3~\cite{dinov3} has demonstrated that models pretrained on very large sets of diverse web imagery are capable of matching or even exceeding Earth-observation-specific foundation models (e.g. DINOv3-sat) on high-resolution RGB RS tasks~\cite{dinov3}. 



Strong self-supervised features present the intriguing possibility of leveraging the encoding of annotated boxes in representation space to learn by virtue of repeated similarities across annotation boxes. This is conceptually similar to inferring the subject of a list of verbal representations (Figure~\ref{fig:concept}): ``ash'', ``trunk'', ``dirt'', ``car'', ``leaves'', ``birch'', ``canopy'', ``branches'' etc. Clearly challenges remain, not least of which is filtering out unwanted elements such as ``dirt'' and ``car'', and to produce a reliable model for the specific task of TCIS is the main aim of this work. In other words, we effectively ask the question: ``can we leverage the vector space signature of the tree-ness within DINO latent feature vectors, based only on the commonality between GT boxes, to isolate the trees from the background?'' Furthermore, this approach needs to work without the model ever being told which elements of the bounding boxes it should be targeting: it is completely tree-agnostic in principle, and only becomes a TCIS model through training exposure.

\paragraph{Contribution}

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item LACE, a box-supervised instance segmentation model whose novel expectation-\hspace{0pt}maximisation box-to-mask module isolates crowns in frozen DINOv3-web (ViT-L/16)~\cite{dinov3} features from the latent commonality of the GT boxes alone, paired with a DINO (DETR)~\cite{dinodetr} Swin-L~\cite{swin} detector trained on the same boxes. Given identical detector boxes, the module outperforms the mask-pretrained foundation model SAM~3 at AP$_{50}$ (Section~\ref{sec:samebox}).
\item A benchmark comparison of the key box- and mask-supervised state-of-the-art (SOTA) models in the TCIS space, every one re-run and re-scored by us under a single protocol (Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}). On OAM-TCD, which spans a diverse range of biomes, tree species and background contexts, LACE outperforms both the existing box-supervised models and best-in-class mask-supervised models, including the OAM-TCD authors' Mask R-CNN trained on several times more images, with a relative mask AP$_{50}$ gain of $7.5\%$ or more (Table~\ref{tab:oamtcd439}).
\end{itemize}


\section{Related Work}

In recent years there has been a focus in the RS community on RGB datasets which can directly leverage Computer Vision deep learning models~\cite{zhu2017} especially through the use of Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs)~\cite{lecun1998,alexnet} and Vision Transformers (ViT)~\cite{vit}. Mask R-CNN~\cite{maskrcnn}, the instance segmentation member of the region-based CNN (R-CNN) family of models~\cite{rcnn}, extends the Faster R-CNN~\cite{fasterrcnn} detector with a parallel per-instance mask head and is a common architecture for TCIS, including two of our baselines, Detectree2~\cite{ball2023} and Restor's OAM-TCD model~\cite{oamtcd}, both fine-tuned from Detectron2~\cite{detectron2} checkpoints trained on COCO~\cite{coco} from ImageNet-1k-pretrained~\cite{imagenet} backbones. Alternative approaches such as Star-convex representation have also been explored to improve performance on dense canopy \cite{tong2025, fgtreeseg}.


\paragraph{Segment Anything Model (SAM)}

Meta's Segment Anything Model (SAM)~\cite{sam}, now in its third iteration~\cite{sam3}, is a promptable class-agnostic segmentation foundation model. In remote sensing, SAM has been benchmarked zero-shot on satellite imagery~\cite{ren2024space}, and RSPrompter~\cite{rsprompter} overcomes SAM's reliance on prompts such as points/boxes/masks by using a trained prompter to generate prompt embeddings for aerial imagery directly. 
SAM has further been applied to TCIS either as a box-to-mask module prompted by a separate detector or centre predictor~\cite{fmsam,chen2025zeroshot,treepseco}, or adapted to the domain by fine-tuning, side-tuning or elevation inputs~\cite{teng2025,treesam,ginkgo}, the latter motivated by poor out-of-the-box performance~\cite{teng2025,treesam}.

\paragraph{Self-DIstillation with NO labels (DINO)}

DINOv3~\cite{dinov3}, the latest of Meta's DINO family~\cite{dino,dinov2}, is a SOTA encoder trained with fully Self-Supervised Learning (SSL) on $1.7$ billion images. Highly context-agnostic, DINOv3-web is an example of a frozen SSL backbone that can serve as a ``universal visual encoder''~\cite{dinov3}.


\paragraph{Box-supervised mask inference}

Existing approaches fall broadly into two paradigms. The first is loss design with hand-crafted objectives computed from the box and low-level image cues. BoxInst~\cite{boxinst} established the modern approach with a projection loss requiring the projections of the predicted mask to match the box edges and a pairwise affinity loss encouraging neighbouring pixels of similar colour to share a label, reaching $33.2$ mask AP on COCO~\cite{coco} against $39.1$ for its fully mask-supervised counterpart. BoxSnake~\cite{boxsnake} instead supervises polygon vertices directly, and Box2Mask~\cite{box2mask} evolves a level-set function inside each box with an energy over both the image and its deep features, reaching $42.4$ mask AP on COCO test-dev with a Swin-L~\cite{swin} backbone. The second paradigm is to utilise mask predictions for self-training. An early example is ``Simple Does It''~\cite{khoreva2017}, which generated pseudo-masks inside boxes with GrabCut~\cite{rother2004} and MCG~\cite{pont2017mcg} and recursively trained a segmentation network on them. In BoxTeacher~\cite{boxteacher}, a teacher predicts confidence-weighted pseudo-masks that supervise the student alongside BoxInst's losses, and is updated as a moving average of the student. DiscoBox~\cite{discobox} instead uses a structured energy teacher whose pairwise and cross-image potentials refine masks within boxes. We use one representative of each paradigm, Box2Mask and BoxTeacher, as baselines (Table~\ref{tab:oamtcd439}); to the best of our knowledge, trained box-supervised instance segmentation architectures have not been previously explored for TCIS.

LACE represents a third way, instead building on self-supervised embeddings to derive statistical relationships of in-box characteristics. Unlike EM-Adapt~\cite{emadapt}, which amortises the E-step into a gradient-trained CNN with implicit cross-box sharing, we fit an explicit closed-form commonality mixture over frozen features. This thematically follows DiscoBox~\cite{discobox} and DDT~\cite{ddt} in exploiting similarities between embeddings within GT boxes, but aligns more closely with STEGO~\cite{stego}, eschewing pixel-space calculations in favour of mask inference in latent space.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:LACE}

Our method produces instance masks for individual tree crowns using bounding-box supervision only. It has three stages: an object detector trained on bounding boxes (Section~\ref{sec:detector}); a dense feature grid obtained by interlacing sixteen offset passes of a frozen DINOv3-web ViT (Section~\ref{sec:features}); and a novel box-to-mask module that converts each predicted box into a mask (Section~\ref{sec:masker}). Predictions are then ranked by a mask-aware confidence that combines the detector score with the mask module's own posterior, refined by a small learned scorer $\hat s$ (Section~\ref{sec:scorer}). Figure~\ref{fig:architecture} shows inference and Figure~\ref{fig:masker-fit} the one-off fit. Further details are given in Appendix~\ref{app:method}.

\subsection{Box detector}
\label{sec:detector}

%CHECK canopy-ignore wording vs dino_det/README.md (unmatched query, box >= 50% canopy, no negative cls loss)
Detection uses DINO~\cite{dinodetr}, a DETR-style detector, with a Swin-L~\cite{swin} backbone. We initialise it from generic COCO-trained weights and train it with a single tree class on the bounding boxes of the $792$ training images only. Where OAM-TCD marks a region as canopy, a predicted box with $\geq 50\%$ of its area inside the canopy is ignored, mirroring the crowd rule used in evaluation. Each $2048$~px tile is processed as a $3 \times 3$ grid of overlapping $1024$~px subtiles. Boxes scoring below $0.05$ are dropped. The detector score $s$ of each box is DINO's class probability. Optimisation and stitching details are given in Table~\ref{tab:config}.

\subsection{Dense features by phase interlacing}
\label{sec:features}

A ViT with patch size $p$ emits one embedding per $p \times p$ patch, giving a feature grid $p$ times coarser than the image. We evaluate the frozen encoder sixteen times per tile, shifting the input by $(\delta_y, \delta_x) \in \{0, p/4, p/2, 3p/4\}^2$, and interlace the sixteen grids. With $p = 16$ this yields a $4$~px lattice, or in other words, building a lattice of 16px embeddings each centered on a separate 4px patch. Writing $F^{(\delta_y,\delta_x)}$ for the grid from one offset, the assembled feature tensor $\widetilde F$ is

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\widetilde F\!\left[:,\; 4i + \tfrac{\delta_y}{4},\; 4j + \tfrac{\delta_x}{4}\right] &= F^{(\delta_y,\delta_x)}[:,\, i,\, j], \\
i, j &\in \{0, \dots, 127\}
\end{split}
\label{eq:interlace}
\end{equation}
% verified 2026-11-04 against native_raster/hunt/feat16p.py: Z[dy//4::4, dx//4::4] = phase (dy,dx); patch (gy,gx) of phase (dy,dx) centred at pixel (dx+8+16gx, dy+8+16gy), i.e. cell (Y,X) -> (4X+8, 4Y+8).

so that cell $(Y, X)$ of $\widetilde F$ carries the embedding of the patch centred at pixel $(4X + 8,\, 4Y + 8)$. Each cell is therefore the encoder output at its own location, rather than any kind of interpolation of its neighbours. The box-to-mask module is fitted on the $8$~px lattice of the four offsets $\{0, p/2\}^2$. All reported results use sixteen offsets at inference, but four offsets, interpolated to the $4$~px lattice, match them at AP$_{50}$ with a quarter of the encoder passes (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}), and we recommend four as the default.

Encoding uses DINOv3 ViT-L/16 with weights frozen throughout. On OAM-TCD we take final-layer (layer~$24$) activations ($1024$ dimensions), giving $\widetilde F \in \mathbb{R}^{1024 \times 512 \times 512}$ for a $2048$~px tile.

\subsection{Box-to-mask module}
\label{sec:masker}

Given a detection box, the task remains to decide which of its cells belong to the crown. The challenge is that a valid box will almost always contain pixels of the ground or surrounding objects as well, simply because trees tend not to be perfect rectangular boxes in shape. We treat this as foreground/background discrimination in a whitened feature space, with the model fitted once on training tiles (using only boxes; Algorithm~\ref{alg:fit}) and then held fixed.

The module has four key components: a whitened feature space; a frozen background mixture; a set of foreground prototypes; and a spatial prior over relative position within the box. The last two are learned by an alternating EM-based fit, while the first two stay fixed.

\paragraph{Feature space}
LACE's core idea is to treat the rich DINOv3-web embeddings as sufficiently descriptive representations of each patch to build a high-performing tree-crown instance segmentation model. We begin with $a$, a given cell's raw unmodified embedding, and PCA-whiten it to $d = 128$ dimensions. A final $L_2$ normalisation then places the result on the unit sphere, giving the unit vector $z$.

\begin{equation}
\tilde z \;=\; \frac{(a - \mu)\,W}{\lambda}, \qquad z \;=\; \frac{\tilde z}{\lVert \tilde z \rVert}
\label{eq:whiten}
\end{equation}

Here $a \in \mathbb{R}^{1024}$ is one cell of $\widetilde F$; $\mu \in \mathbb{R}^{1024}$ is the cell mean, and $W \in \mathbb{R}^{1024 \times d}$ is the whitening matrix, whose columns are the retained principal directions. $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ is the vector of per-component standard deviations, which are all estimated once on training cells and then frozen. The unit norm makes all subsequent comparisons simple cosine similarities and allows both foreground and background to be modelled by von Mises--Fisher components~\cite{banerjee2005vmf} with a shared concentration $\kappa$.

\paragraph{Background mixture}
Cells outside every box, excluding OAM-TCD's canopy class, are background. A background mixture of $K_{bg}$ components $\{(w_j, G_j)\}_{j=1}^{K_{bg}}$ is fitted to them by spherical $k$-means and \emph{frozen}, giving the background log-likelihood $\ell_{bg}$ as follows:

\begin{equation}
\ell_{bg}(z) \;=\; \log \textstyle\sum_j w_j \exp\!\left(\kappa\, z^{\top} G_j\right)
\label{eq:bgll}
\end{equation}

The log-sum-exp acts as a smooth maximum over the components, so $\ell_{bg}(z)$ is a single scalar per cell reading as how well that cell matches its best-matching background component.

\paragraph{Foreground mixture}
Foreground takes the same von Mises--Fisher form, but its weights are supplied per cell by the spatial prior and re-estimated at every M-step, as opposed to global and frozen. Foreground is represented by $K_{fg}$ unit prototypes $\{C_k\}_{k=1}^{K_{fg}}$, initialised from in-box cells atypical of the background. This is merely a heuristic to kick-start the fitting. Without this restriction, the initialisation is seeded from in-box background elements such as soil and roads, which are numerous and not the intended crown foreground.

\paragraph{Spatial prior}
We observe that a crown typically occupies a roughly central, roughly circular region of a reasonably tight GT box. We encode this as a spatial prior $\rho$ over the crown's \emph{relative} position within the box, on an $N \times N$ grid of bins in normalised box coordinates $(u, v) \in [0,1]^2$, so $\rho$ is by construction invariant to crown size, tile size and ground sample distance. $\rho_k(b)$ is the prior that a cell in bin $b$ is crown and also explained by prototype $k$. $\hat\rho_k = \rho_k / \sum_{k'} \rho_{k'}$ normalises it over the $K_{fg}$ prototypes, while the unnormalised total $\sum_k \rho_k$ remains the probability that the bin is crown at all. $\rho$ is initialised flat, and at every M-step its mean per-bin foreground mass is rescaled to a nominal $\pi/4 \approx 0.785$, the area fraction of an ellipse inscribed in its bounding box for any aspect ratio (realised $0.690$ after the per-bin cap, Appendix~\ref{app:method}).

\paragraph{Fitting}
\medskip\noindent\emph{E-step.} For the cells of one box, with $\rho_k$ the spatial prior evaluated at each cell's bin for prototype $k$ and $\sum_k \rho_k$ the total foreground prior at that bin:

\begin{align}
A(z) &\;=\; \log \textstyle\sum_k \hat\rho_k \exp\!\left(\kappa\, z^{\top} C_k\right) \;-\; \ell_{bg}(z)
\label{eq:logratio}\\[2pt]
\bar A(z) &\;=\; A(z) - \operatorname{mean}_{z' \in \mathrm{box}} A(z')
\label{eq:meancentre}\\[2pt]
p_{fg}(z) &\;=\; \sigma\!\left(\bar A(z) \;+\; \alpha \, \mathrm{logit}\!\left(\textstyle\sum_k \rho_k\right)\right)
\label{eq:posterior}
\end{align}

Equation~\eqref{eq:logratio} is a log-likelihood ratio. Its first term is the foreground log-likelihood, built from the prototypes $C_k$ exactly as Equation~\eqref{eq:bgll} is built from the background centroids $G_j$; subtracting $\ell_{bg}$ gives $A(z) = \log\!\big(p(z \mid \text{crown}) / p(z \mid \text{background})\big)$. A cell must therefore look crown-like \emph{and} unlike background to score highly. This removes the soil/ground that resembles the background mixture outright.

Equation~\eqref{eq:meancentre} is a second filter. Problematically, attention over the surrounding tile inflates the absolute foreground score of \emph{every} cell inside a box, so $A$ is confounded by context and its absolute level is not sufficiently informative (Table~\ref{tab:components}): e.g.\ soil surrounded by canopy can still score positively, and therefore survive the first filter. However, mean-centring (subtracting the box mean of $A$ from each cell) isolates $A$ \emph{within} a box, asking not whether a cell is crown-like but whether it is \emph{more} crown-like than the rest of its own box. The behaviour that follows is essentially precisely what we wanted to produce: a heterogeneous box containing crown and soil has a wide spread of $\bar A$ and is carved appropriately, whereas a box in uniformly closed canopy has a narrow spread and stays closer to filled, which is generally correct.

Alongside $p_{fg}$, the E-step returns a responsibility $r_{nk}$ for each cell $n$ of the box (with whitened embedding $z_n$) and each prototype $k$:

\begin{equation}
r_{nk} \;=\; p_{fg}(z_n)\;
\frac{\rho_k \exp\!\left(\kappa\, z_n^{\top} C_k\right)}
     {\sum_{k'} \rho_{k'} \exp\!\left(\kappa\, z_n^{\top} C_{k'}\right)}
\label{eq:resp}
\end{equation}

which together distribute each cell's foreground probability over the $K_{fg}$ prototypes in proportion to the weighted components of Equation~\eqref{eq:logratio}, so that $\sum_k r_{nk} = p_{fg}(z_n)$.

\medskip\noindent\emph{M-step.} The prototypes are updated in two steps. The first is the ordinary generative pull: prototype $k$ is drawn towards $P_k$, the unit-normalised responsibility-weighted mean $\sum_n r_{nk}\, z_n$ of all cells over all boxes, which expresses the idea ``recurs across boxes''. The second move repels the prototype from the outside-box signature it most resembles. The background cells are softmax-assigned to the current prototypes at the same concentration $\kappa$, with cells in a one-cell ring around each box weighted twice: they share illumination, phenology and local context (e.g.\ ground shadow) with the crown and so are the hardest negatives. The resulting per-prototype negative centroid $\bar N_k$ is the normalised weighted mean of the cells assigned to prototype $k$, and the prototype becomes

\begin{equation}
C_k \;=\; \frac{P_k - \beta\,\bar N_k}{\lVert P_k - \beta\,\bar N_k \rVert}
\label{eq:contrastive}
\end{equation}

A soil-like prototype's nearest negatives are themselves soil, so the subtraction tears it away from soil: commonality is defined as agreement-across-boxes \emph{minus} outside-box signature, not merely whatever fills the box. The contrastive weight $\beta$ controls the strength of that repulsion; $\beta = 0$ recovers the purely generative update $C_k = P_k$ (compared in Appendix~\ref{app:ablations}), and we use $\beta = 0.5$ throughout. The spatial prior is re-estimated from the same responsibilities: $\tilde\rho_k(b)$ is the mean of $r_{nk}$ over the cells $n$ falling in bin $b$, then constrained as described above to give $\rho$. Without this prior ($\alpha = 0$), test mask AP$_{50}$ falls by $0.022$ (Table~\ref{tab:masker}).

Fitting is deterministic given the seed. The module is fitted on the $8$~px lattice, a subset of the $4$~px lattice, and applied unchanged on the $4$~px lattice. We found that refitting on the $4$~px lattice, with $120$ or $250$ training tiles, only changes validation mask AP$_{50}$ by at most $0.001$, consistent with both fits sampling the same feature distribution.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Box-to-mask fit (once, boxes only; $\kappa = 10$, $\beta = 0.5$, $T = 30$).\label{alg:fit}}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State Whiten and normalise every cell's feature to a unit vector $z$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:whiten}).
\State Learn what background looks like: cluster outside-box cells into a background mixture and \textbf{freeze} it (Equation~\eqref{eq:bgll}).
\State Seed crown prototypes $C$ from in-box cells least like background; start the spatial prior $\rho$ flat.
\For{$t = 1$ \textbf{to} $T$}
  \State Score each in-box cell as crown or not ($p_{fg}$): more crown-like than background, relative to its own box, and in a likely position; share that score among the prototypes ($r_{nk}$) (Equations~\eqref{eq:logratio}--\eqref{eq:resp}). \Comment{E-step}
  \State Pull each prototype towards the in-box cells it explains, across all boxes, and push it away from the outside-box cells it most resembles (Equation~\eqref{eq:contrastive}). \Comment{M-step}
  \State Re-estimate where in a box crowns tend to sit ($\rho$), rescaling its total mass to $\pi/4$.
\EndFor
\State \Return whitening, frozen background, prototypes $C$, spatial prior $\rho$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}


\subsection{Inference}
\label{sec:inference}

The posterior $p_{fg}$ of each predicted box lives on the $4$~px feature grid, so we upsample it to the native $2048$~px tile with a guided filter~\cite{guidedfilter} that uses the RGB tile as its guide, so that mask boundaries follow edges in the image rather than the feature grid. Pixels above a mask threshold $\tau$ form the predicted crown mask, which is intersected with the box, so a mask is contained in its box by construction rather than by penalty. $\tau$ is set per box to $0.33$ times the maximum of the filtered posterior inside the box, so that small crowns under-covered by imprecise predicted boxes are recovered. The prior weight $\alpha = 0.3$ scales the box-derived spatial term in Equation~\eqref{eq:posterior}: values $< 1$ relax reliance on a \emph{predicted} box, which is not always precise, so that the appearance term dominates. The concentration $\kappa$ is raised from $10$ at fitting to $16$ at inference in Equations~\eqref{eq:bgll} and~\eqref{eq:logratio}, sharpening the appearance term $\bar A$, which is zero-mean within each box.


\subsection{Confidence from the mask posterior}
\label{sec:scorer}

The detector score $s$ (Section~\ref{sec:detector}) rates whether a crown is present in the box, not how good the resulting mask (Section~\ref{sec:masker}) is. To generate a confidence score we can reduce the posterior over the $\lvert B \rvert$ cells of a box $B$ to two terms.

\begin{align}
\bar p(B) &\;=\; \frac{1}{\lvert B \rvert} \sum_{n \in B} p_{fg}(z_n)
\label{eq:meanpost}\\[2pt]
D(B) &\;=\; \frac{1}{\lvert B \rvert} \sum_{n \in B}
              \bigl\lvert\, 2\,p_{fg}(z_n) - 1 \,\bigr\rvert
\label{eq:decisive}
\end{align}

Because the mean-centring of Equation~\eqref{eq:meancentre} sets the mean of $\bar A$ to zero within every box, both statistics read the \emph{shape} of the within-box posterior rather than its level. Read this way $\bar p$ is a soft estimate of the fraction of its box that the crown occupies. The decisiveness $D$ folds each cell to its distance from $\tfrac12$ before averaging, and so records the \emph{spread} of the same distribution: $D = 1$ when every cell is confidently inside or outside, $D = 0$ when every cell is a coin flip.

\paragraph{Learned confidence} A small MLP gives the final mask-aware confidence $\hat s$. It takes four inputs per detection: $s$, $\log(\bar p\, D)$, the log box height $\log h$, and $\mathrm{IoU}_{\uparrow}$, the largest box IoU with any higher-scoring detection, which marks likely duplicates. It has one hidden layer of 64 units, trained on the validation detections to predict whether each matches a GT crown at box IoU $\geq 0.5$; detections falling in canopy are excluded, as they are in scoring. The deployed $\hat s$ is the mean of $25$ such MLPs ($5$ seeds $\times$ $5$ validation folds).

\section{Experiments}

\subsection{Datasets}

\paragraph{Restor OAM-TCD}

The OAM-TCD dataset~\cite{oamtcd}, introduced by Veitch-Michaelis et al., uses OpenAerialMap (OAM)~\cite{oam} RGB imagery and provides $5072$ $2048\times2048$~px images at $0.1$~m/px resolution with associated human-labelled instance masks for over $280$k individual and $56$k groups of trees (``canopy''). The OAM-TCD dataset provides polygon masks, as well as a broad range of ecological biomes, also including examples of the savannah \& dryland landscapes so scarce in existing annotated datasets. To our knowledge it is the only public dataset with individual crown masks spanning sparse-canopy biomes, and is therefore the only benchmark on which sparse-canopy TCIS can be measured.

Of the $5072$ images in the OAM-TCD dataset, $439$ are reserved as a holdout benchmark test set, allowing for a global multi-biome comparison between models (Table~\ref{tab:test-biomes}, Appendix~\ref{app:datasets}). The $439$-image test set contains $25{,}705$ ITC annotations, and $366$ images within the set contain $\geq 1$ crown, i.e., $73$ of the images have $0$ ITCs. The same test set also contains $6{,}782$ canopy annotations, which are groups of trees in a wide range of real-world sizes. During creation of the dataset, human annotators were told to use the canopy class when it was not possible to reliably delineate individual trees, including when it was ``not obvious whether a tree was an individual or multiple''. The densest tile holds $422$ individual crowns, and the median tile holds $29$.

\paragraph{Tree Crown Instance Segmentation}

For TCIS, we restrict our evaluation to OAM-TCD's Tree class (cat=2). One approach in the literature~\cite{selvabox}, which we agree with and utilise in this work, is to ignore all canopy annotations (cat=1) altogether. Unsurprisingly, the model's predictions often fall inside areas marked as canopy, which contain entirely valid but merged tree crowns, but we exclude these from the evaluation metrics entirely (using COCO's iscrowd=1 to ignore). The rationale here is that the annotating of canopy into groups leads to subjective groupings. For example, should a clump of $10$ tree crowns be best delineated by $1$ canopy of $10$ crowns, or $2$ of $5$? We claim both are equally valid, and by using subjective groupings, metrics such as AP and F1 become corrupted since $\mathrm{IoU}>0.X$ is based on agreement of delineations. Every OAM-TCD figure we report is therefore ``canopy-neutral'', unless otherwise specified.

\subsection{Ablation Studies}
\label{sec:ablation}

We ablate the two central contributions, the box-to-mask module and the mask-aware confidence $\hat s$, and the interlaced feature grid that feeds them. Unless stated otherwise, ablations of the module hold the detector fixed, so every arm scores the identical set of predicted boxes and any difference is attributable to mask generation alone. All ablations use a single detector run, and intervals are paired tile-bootstrap $95\%$ intervals. Further ablations of the module (hyperparameters, dense canopy and foreground collapse) are given in Appendix~\ref{app:ablations}.

\paragraph{Feature grid: interlacing versus interpolation}

LACE interlaces offset passes of the frozen encoder into a real $4$~px grid (Section~\ref{sec:features}). With detections, module and scorer held fixed (Table~\ref{tab:grid}), interlacing beats interpolation on every metric, modestly at AP$_{50}$ ($+0.003$) and more at AP$_{75}$ ($+0.014$), where boundary placement matters most. Interpolation adds sampling resolution but no new evidence, whereas the offsets add evidence. Four offsets $\{0, p/2\}^2$ match sixteen at AP$_{50}$ ($0.000$, $[-0.001, 0.002]$) and trail by $0.001$ at AP$_{50:95}$. The gain from interlacing is therefore almost entirely that of the first four offsets.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{A note of caution}
In a warming world, it is tempting to reduce the objective to something along the lines of `tree maximalism', i.e.\ the position that more trees is always better. Open African savannah in particular, as argued by Bond et al.~\cite{bond2019}, has often been considered as a kind of deforested or degraded landscape~\cite{laestadius2012,bastin2019} when in fact the biodiversity~\cite{veldman2015,jenkins2013}, surface temperature through biophysical effects~\cite{bright2015}, streamflow~\cite{jackson2005}, and vulnerability to fire~\cite{veldman2015} are all negatively impacted by aggressive tree \emph{planting} in these environments, and moreover Dass et al.~\cite{dass2018} have argued that grasslands (in California) may actually be more reliable and robust carbon sinks than dense forests. Further analysis of this debate is beyond the scope of this work, but we call attention to the nuance required in practical implementation of tree crown delineation and carbon accounting using techniques such as LACE.

\paragraph{TCIS}
LACE's competitive results against other box-supervised models and fully supervised models promise a new viable option for TCIS tasks utilising time- and cost-efficient box annotations. It has demonstrated efficacy over a wide range of biomes, due to the flexibility of self-supervised embeddings to identify a diverse range of tree crowns, and is particularly useful for sparse-canopy environments where the focus is on measuring tree crown count, size, and allometries over large, noisy landscapes. 

% source (verified 2026-11-16): dino_det/results/guidectl/guidectl_test.json (pre-registration GC-test, R3 rows, test 439): guided - bilinear AP75 +0.0255, AP50:95 +0.0120, AP50 +0.0043; LACE AP75 0.2713. Restor AP75 0.2968 = native_raster/scored_restor_rpn1000_r2048_tree.json (canopy_neutral_crowd)
By taking the $4$~px interlaced grid as the unit of crown delineation, LACE has a disadvantage relative to pixel-based maskers: its posterior is defined on a grid four times coarser than the image. The guided upsampling of Section~\ref{sec:inference} compensates for this at the native raster by letting the image decide where a boundary falls between cells. On the test set it adds $+0.026$ AP$_{75}$ and $+0.012$ AP$_{50:95}$ over bilinear upsampling (AP$_{50}$ $+0.004$), while a blur with no image guide and the RGB of a different tile both fall below bilinear on every metric, so the gain comes from the image's own edges rather than from smoothing. LACE leads the Mask R-CNN on AP$_{50:95}$ ($0.342$ against $0.333$) but still trails it at certain strict IoUs (e.g.\ AP$_{75}$ $0.271$ against $0.297$). This is a key trade-off of using a masker that has not been trained on polygon masks.

It should also be emphasised that the box-supervised models have an additional, subtle disadvantage to their mask-supervised counterparts. Human annotations function as the ground truth and therefore any manually annotated dataset naturally contains idiosyncrasies and a degree of interpretation in its annotations, particularly with respect to edge cases and ambiguities. Mask-supervised models are able to learn these characteristics to some degree at least, whereas models within the box-supervised category are measured by an unseen yardstick, and are therefore highly susceptible to poor-quality annotations. They are fully reliant on agreement between the actual ground truth and the dataset's version of GT, having not had any opportunity to adjust to a given dataset's biases. We present our results as demonstration of the efficacy of LACE, and also of the high quality of the OAM-TCD annotations, since it would simply not be possible to compete with a Mask R-CNN were the tree crown polygons poorly drawn. 

\paragraph{Spatial prior}
Although the spatial prior is maintained separately for three crown-size bands (Appendix~\ref{app:method}), in practice we observe very little difference between the three fitted priors, contrary to our expectations. We leave the stratification in as a defensive component that does not add any significant complexity, but acknowledge that a single pooled prior would likely perform comparably.

\paragraph{Crown annotations in dense canopy}
Existing datasets that try to provide instance segmentation labels very often will tend to attempt to draw definitive `ground truth' on dense canopy where there arguably is simply not enough information in the image to make that determination definitively~\cite{ball2023,steier2024}. Firstly, where GT annotations leave non-trivial gaps between annotation boundaries, a model that produces more inclusive, justified boundaries can be penalised. Secondly, in annotated dense canopy, the resulting benchmark contains some subjectivity, i.e., many predictions that are defensible and possibly even more accurate than the ground truth can be counted as False Positives (FP). This is particularly an issue with RGB imagery that includes trees of varying scales i.e. smaller and larger, as a mismatched scale between prediction and GT leads to much higher FP rates since (m)AP$_{50}$ is highly sensitive to scale mismatch for purely geometric reasons. If one of the two is less than $50\%$ of the area of the other, it will be counted as a FP regardless of placement. In contexts such as dense canopy where, for example, a single large crown could be one tree or two trees, the metric is sensitive to the arbitrary choice of division.

\paragraph{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
\item LACE is evaluated end-to-end on a single mask benchmark, OAM-TCD, at $0.1$~m/px RGB; its behaviour at coarser GSD, or with multispectral or LiDAR inputs, is untested.
\item At strict IoU its boundaries trail those of mask-trained models (AP$_{75}$ $0.271$ against $0.297$ for Restor's Mask R-CNN), since the masker never sees a polygon.
\item The box-to-mask module transfers to objects a few encoder patches across (Table~\ref{tab:phenobench}), but not to objects of about one patch, as on LIVECell (Table~\ref{tab:phenobench-choice}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

In this study we present LACE, a model, tree-agnostic in design, for instance segmentation in box-supervised Tree Crown tasks, where annotations are scarce. Our box-to-mask inference method, by leveraging the statistical signature of the DINOv3 latent composite, provides a SOTA-competitive solution to true TCIS without the need for time-consuming and/or expensive polygon masks. With this contribution, we hope to support the Remote Sensing community in the previously underexplored area of TCIS in diverse global biomes, particularly sparse-canopy landscapes.

\subsection{Method details}
\label{app:method}

\paragraph{Interlaced grid}
Registration of the interlaced grid of Equation~\eqref{eq:interlace} is verified independently: a synthetic blob is localised to within one cell across $200$ positions, and the cells of $\widetilde F$ belonging to the unshifted phase reproduce a plain single-pass extraction to floating-point precision, confirming its indexing.

\paragraph{Background mixture}
Note that the standard von Mises--Fisher normalising constant $C_d(\kappa)$ is omitted from Equation~\eqref{eq:bgll} and from the foreground term of Equation~\eqref{eq:logratio} alike; sharing a single concentration parameter $\kappa$ between the two mixtures makes it identical on both sides, so it cancels in the difference and never has to be evaluated in $d = 128$ dimensions.

\paragraph{Spatial prior}
Separate priors are kept for three crown-size bands (training box-size terciles), since at a fixed feature stride the number of cells spanning a crown depends on its size; each predicted box is masked with the prior of its band. Each bin is also capped at $0.95$, so no relative position is ever treated as certainly foreground.
Pinning the mass to a constant derived from geometry rather than fitted is what keeps the prior label-free, and it means the fit decides only \emph{where} the foreground mass sits, never \emph{how much} of it there is: without the constraint $\rho$ inflates towards unity, stops discriminating position, and the $\alpha$ term of Equation~\eqref{eq:posterior} degenerates into an additive constant.

\paragraph{Fitting}
Strictly speaking, the procedure is EM-style rather than EM proper: the within-box mean-centring of Equation~\eqref{eq:meancentre}, the contrastive term of Equation~\eqref{eq:contrastive} and the projection of $\rho$ onto the mass and cap constraints each break the correspondence with a joint likelihood, so no objective is monotonically ascended and there is no convergence test: $T$ is fixed at $30$ iterations.
The constraints are applied as a single rescale followed by the cap, so the cap partly undoes the rescaling: on the deployed fit this leaves a realised mean per-bin mass of $0.690$ against the nominal $0.785$.

\paragraph{Learned confidence}
The four inputs are what remained after backward elimination from fourteen candidates on validation; tile-level context features were the first removed.

\subsection{Supervision and model selection}
\label{app:selection}

%CHECK masker fit: 120 training tiles (tab:config); canopy raster derives from canopy-class polygons
No crown polygon is read at any stage of encoding, detection or mask fitting. OAM-TCD's canopy class, which marks groups of trees that annotators could not separate, enters only as a region to ignore. Mask labels are used only on the $108$ validation images held out from the $900$ training images. The prior weight $\alpha$, the concentration $\kappa$, the mask threshold $\tau$ and the radius and $\epsilon$ of the guided filter are selected there, on the boxes of an earlier detector trained on the same images, and were frozen before the DINO detector was trained; none of these requires refitting. The $4$~px lattice, the box-relative $\tau$ and ranking by the $4$~px posterior were chosen by a hyperparameter sweep of ${\sim}170$ configurations on the same validation images. The learned confidence $\hat s$ is trained on the validation detections with box-match labels and was selected by validation mask AP$_{50}$.

\paragraph{Dense canopy}

Foreground/background commonality within a box rests to some extent on the assumption that the box contains mostly one crown, meaning crowded trees with overlapping crowns could be a challenge. 


% source (2026-11-10): ablation/results_r2048/strata_instance_d1_r2048.json (pre-registration ABL-D1 item 2), LACE Table 1 predictions, box-matched arm
We call a crown \emph{touching} when its GT box overlaps that of another crown, which happens to be the case for $39.3\%$ of the $25{,}705$ test crowns. Touching and isolated crowns score almost identically: mean mask IoU $0.7353$ against $0.7330$, and touching crowns fail slightly more often ($0.0241$ versus $0.0223$, where failure is a box-matched true positive whose mask IoU falls below $0.5$). Touching crowns are also larger (mean box side $\sqrt{wh}$ of $68.9$ versus $42.0$~px), which may partly offset neighbour interference.

\paragraph{Foreground collapse} 
The module was designed as a mixture of $K_{fg}$ foreground prototypes, the idea being that different trees could present as distinct prototypes in latent space (e.g. conifers vs palm trees). However, what we found was that with the inclusion of the contrastive term (at a contrastive weight of $\beta = 0.5$), the mixture collapses to a single foreground prototype. Mean pairwise cosine rises smoothly with $\beta$ (Table~\ref{tab:collapse}) to $0.994$ at the deployed $\beta = 0.5$, an effective rank of $1.66$ of $16$. Pruning tests responsibility share, which the highly similar prototypes survive because each contributes more or less equally. In practice, pruning is activated at intermediate $\beta$ where some prototypes do starve.

We deploy $\beta = 0.5$, but Table~\ref{tab:components} shows that $\beta = 0$ with mean-centring is almost as accurate on GT boxes ($0.7950$ against $0.7980$), and the reason is that the two subtractions remove the same thing. Repulsion subtracts the background signature from each prototype once, at the M-step (Equation~\eqref{eq:contrastive}); mean-centring subtracts the box mean from each cell's log-ratio at the E-step (Equation~\eqref{eq:meancentre}), and that mean is dominated by the background the box shares with its surroundings. Mean-centring on its own recovers nearly all of the separation between crown and background, and repulsion on its own recovers under a third of it. Applying both removes little more than mean-centring alone, which is the negative interaction in Table~\ref{tab:components}.
What $\beta = 0.5$ also has is identifiability (i.e. it generates an explicit ``treeness direction''): refit at three EM seeds and at two values of $K_{fg}$ it recovers the same direction every time ($\cos \geq 0.9908$), whereas the $\beta = 0$ mixture spreads the same foreground over sixteen prototypes whose individual identities are not determined by the data. With neither subtraction applied, masks occupy much more of the box corners and mean crown IoU on GT boxes falls to $0.7412$, below either subtraction alone ($0.7585$, $0.7950$).

We theorise that the variance and diversity of OAM-TCD imagery is a key driver of this collapse: it effectively reduces the different tree prototypes, although fairly diverse, to a single common direction in latent space.

\subsection{Asymmetries}

\paragraph{Detection budget} The cap of $600$ detections per tile is our protocol choice. The maximum number of tree crowns in the test set is $422$, leaving comfortably enough room for accurate detections without penalty. Two models emit more than $600$ detections per tile when the cap is removed: Detectree2 emits up to $1{,}333$ at the protocol floor, and LACE up to $1{,}186$, exceeding $600$ on $212$ of the $439$ tiles. Re-scored at $1{,}200$, Detectree2 gains $0.002$ ($0.6297 \rightarrow 0.6321$) mask AP$_{50}$ and LACE $0.005$ ($0.714 \rightarrow 0.719$; AP$_{50:95}$ $0.342 \rightarrow 0.344$), which does not change any ranking order in Table~\ref{tab:oamtcd439} and shows that the residuals are a long tail of low-confidence predictions. Both are reported at the protocol cap, so the cap works against LACE rather than for it. Restor and Box2Mask never reach the cap, with at most $377$ and $502$ detections per tile respectively.

\paragraph{Seeds} LACE's OAM-TCD results use a single detector training run (seed $0$). Restor is a single released checkpoint with no seed variation available. Detectree2, Box2Mask and BoxTeacher are trained by us but we did not run multiseed experiments.

\paragraph{Compute} DINOv3 feature extraction, LACE detector training and inference, masker fitting, and all baseline training and inference ran on an NVIDIA A100 (Modal); LACE's $4$~px features ($16$ offsets) and the SAM~3 zero-shot comparison ran on an Apple M4 Max GPU. Mask rendering, the learned confidence $\hat s$, the validation sweeps, subtile stitching and scoring ran on CPU, and every row of every table is scored by the same deterministic CPU code.
